# Insurance Policy Clause Explainer

Upload an Indian health insurance policy PDF. Find out what could get your claim denied — before you file one.

> **Status: working end to end.** Upload a policy PDF in the browser, get a ranked list of the clauses that could cost you money — each in plain English beside the policy's own wording — and ask what-if questions answered with cited clauses. Runs entirely on a local model; your policy never leaves your machine.

## Results

Measured with `qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q4_K_M` as of M38, on three documents: a 39-clause synthetic IRDAI-style policy written for this repository, Star Health's copy of **Arogya Sanjeevani**, the standard product every Indian health insurer must sell with the same wording, and HDFC ERGO's **my: Optima Secure**, a long retail product (both fetched by script, never committed). Scenario verdicts are a **majority of three samples per case**, each sample asking its questions in its own order, and every answer generated by one runtime, Ollama 0.35.1: an Ollama update alone once moved 10 of 69 verdicts, so the Ollama version is part of every cached answer's key (M17 in the learning log). On Star's wording, 9 of 10 questions gave the same verdict in all three samples; the tenth, a caesarean, was right in every sample of one run and wrong in most samples of runs three days later with nothing changed (Failure 94 in the learning log), so agreement within one run is not treated as proof of a stable answer. **Every scenario row below comes from one full run** of all 109 questions (these sets plus 7 more on Star's wording about treatment abroad, 6/7), three samples each, in a single server session after the last code change: 100 of 109 right, with the nine wrong answers named in the learning log (M38 follow-up). Two of the nine answer a claim the policy refuses as paid: a war injury and treatment in Toronto, both on held-out questions. The per-case breakdown of a single sample is in **[`evals/REPORT.md`](evals/REPORT.md)**, regenerated by `python evals/run_all.py`; it scores the main set 37/40 because it replays one stored answer per question where the table scores the majority of three.

**Only the last row is a promise.** The rest measure how well a 7B model on a laptop performs a hard task, and they are published as they came out — including the ones that are not good.

Detection integrity asks something different: of the quotations the model *invented*, how many were caught and shown as unverified rather than presented as evidence? That is what the grounding design guarantees, and the only number here that would count as a bug if it moved. It is computed by re-verifying every quotation independently rather than by reading the flag the pipeline set — a self-reported guarantee is not a measurement.

**The synthetic number to quote is about 82%, from the held-out batches, not 39/40.** The 40 main cases have been used for many keep-or-revert decisions, and the reasoning prompt contains one of them as its example, so a score on them measures fit as much as skill. The held-out batches were written separately (by the same author as the fixes, so not fully independent), and together score 31 of 38. Batches 1 and 2 have since been read while diagnosing failures, and as of M33 so have batches 3 and 4 (5 of 9 between them, one of those a case M33 fixed after reading it), so no batch is now unseen; the next honest measurement needs newly written questions. The main set fell from 38/40 to 33/40 at the start of M16, in a run that changed the context window, the analysis schema and a prompt example at once; that drop is recorded and not yet explained.

**On a real policy, the question is whether the answer rests on the right clause.** M15 found 9 of 10 verdicts right on Star Health's wording, mostly for the wrong reasons, and the pipeline reading it badly: columns interleaved, page headers as clauses, and the scenario step shown a fraction of the policy. M16 fixed the reading in pipeline order and added a reason to every answer-key case: after it, 7 of 10 verdicts were right, and 5 of 10 cited the clause that decides them, against 1 of 10 before. As of M27 it is 9 of 10 verdicts and 8 of 10 citing the deciding clause: M27's fix for HDFC cost Star's dengue question its citation, recorded as a known problem. M32 restored that citation (a refusal that ignores an unserved waiting period is now asked again), and the same run found the caesarean question unstable, which stood Star at 8 of 10. M33 traced that to the maternity exclusion being read backwards ("this exclusion does not apply to caesarean sections") and added a check for a covered answer that cites an exclusion naming the treatment: 9 of 10 verdicts and 9 of 10 citing the deciding clause. On HDFC's wording, 14 of 14: 11 until M30 gave its annexures their own sections, and 12 until M31 made the consistency check read unserved waiting periods and cover windows, which until then were stated in the prompt and checked by nothing. M27 took its first nine questions from 4 to 7 right, when every claim in the first three years had been refused; an illness inside the 30-day wait is still paid (M28 tested two explanations and refuted both). M24 stopped the model being told that a misfiled definitions block was a 36-month waiting period; one verdict that had been right only because of that false line went wrong, which is recorded rather than hidden (Failure 87 in the learning log). Ten questions on one policy is a probe, not a benchmark, so these describe this system on this document rather than an accuracy for real policies in general. Details in [`docs/LEARNING-LOG.md`](docs/LEARNING-LOG.md#m16--reading-a-real-policy-correctly).

## The problem

Policyholders make uninformed decisions because insurance documents are written in legal and technical language that **obscures** the exclusions and conditions deciding claim outcomes.

That word — *obscures* — is what this project takes seriously. A generic "chat with your PDF" wrapper does not address it, because it can only answer questions you already knew to ask. The clauses that get claims denied are the ones you never thought to ask about: a 36-month waiting period, a room-rent cap that silently shrinks every associated charge, a 24-hour notice requirement buried on page 34 behind a defined term.

So this tool inverts the interaction. **It reads the policy and tells you what will get your claim denied, before you ask.**

## What it produces

**1. A risk-ranked clause map.** Every clause typed (coverage / exclusion / condition / sub-limit / waiting period / definition / procedural), rewritten in plain English, and scored for claim-denial impact.

**2. A scenario simulator.** *"I had knee surgery 8 months after buying this"* → covered / not covered / conditional / **your policy doesn't settle this**, with the exact clauses that decide it and the policy's own wording quoted from each.

That fourth verdict is deliberate. A system that cannot say *"your document does not answer this"* will guess instead, and a confident guess about a claim is the specific harm this tool exists to prevent.

## Design

```
PDF ──> [1 ingest] ──> [2 segment] ──> [3 analyze] ──> [4 score] ──> SQLite
        PyMuPDF        rules-based      LLM batched     pure math
        text+bbox      NO LLM           schema-locked   NO LLM
        +char offsets                   + cached

scenario ──> [5a extract facts] ──> [5b choose clauses] ──> [5c reason] ──> [5d verify]
             LLM, schema              all that fit; long    LLM, id-enum    substring check
                                      policies: LLM picks
                                      in ~3k-token groups
```

**Deterministic wherever possible; a model only where language understanding is genuinely required.** This is what makes a 7B model running on a laptop GPU reliable enough to build on. Stages 2 and 4 contain no LLM at all — the model is never asked to do arithmetic, count pages, or decide where a paragraph ends.

### Grounding is structural, not advisory

A wrong answer here costs someone a real claim, so hallucination is prevented by construction rather than discouraged by prompt wording:

**1. Citation IDs are `enum`-constrained** to exactly the clauses supplied in that prompt. Ollama enforces JSON-Schema enums at the *decoding* level — the sampler masks out every token that could not continue a valid document under the schema. A fabricated citation is therefore unrepresentable in the output grammar, not merely unlikely.

**2. Verbatim span check.** Any text the model quotes must appear as a substring of the cited clause's stored source. Failures are surfaced as *unverified*, never rendered as fact.

Underpinning both: every clause stores character offsets into the document's extracted text, with the invariant `raw_text[start:end] == clause.text` asserted by tests. A slice cannot drift from what it is a slice of, the way a copy can.

### Choices that look like omissions

- **No vector DB, no embeddings, no BM25.** Showing the model every clause that could matter beats retrieval on recall, at zero infrastructure cost: retrieval can only drop the clause that decides the case. The model runs with a 28,672-token context, the largest measured to run entirely on this laptop's 8GB GPU, so the standard IRDAI wording (~22,600 tokens) fits in one prompt. **Measured in M16, fitting is not the same as being read**: at that length this 7B model loses track of which clause says what. So a policy with more than ~6,000 tokens of clauses is read in two passes: the model picks the clauses that bear on the question from groups of ~3,000 tokens, then answers from its picks plus every clause the deterministic checks name. Every clause is still read by the model; nothing is ranked by similarity. A wording too long even for the window falls back to a shortlist that gives up definitions and procedural clauses first.
- **No task queue.** `BackgroundTasks` + polling. Single-user local app.
- **No cloud API.** Runs entirely on a local Ollama model. Your policy document never leaves your machine.

## Running it

**Requirements:** Python 3.12+, [Ollama](https://ollama.com), ~6GB free VRAM or RAM.

```bash
ollama pull qwen2.5:7b-instruct-q4_K_M

# Ollama server settings: set these where Ollama will see them (on Windows, as
# user environment variables), then restart Ollama.
#   OLLAMA_FLASH_ATTENTION=1
#   OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPE=q8_0    # halves the context's memory; the app assumes it
#   LLAMA_ARG_CACHE_RAM=1024     # caps the server's saved-prompt store at 1 GB

cd api
python -m venv .venv
.venv/Scripts/python.exe -m pip install -r requirements.txt   # Windows
# .venv/bin/pip install -r requirements.txt                   # macOS/Linux

.venv/Scripts/python.exe -m uvicorn app.main:app --reload
```

Then open <http://localhost:8000/health> to confirm Ollama is reachable and the model is present, or <http://localhost:8000/docs> for the API.

**The web interface** runs alongside it:

```bash
cd web
npm install
npm run dev          # http://localhost:5173
```

Upload a policy and it will show you, in order, the clauses most likely to cost you money - each in plain English, with the policy's own wording and page number beside it, and a line explaining why you would have missed it.

### Tests

```bash
cd api
.venv/Scripts/python.exe -m pytest -v            # all 251 tests
.venv/Scripts/python.exe -m pytest -m "not llm"  # the 243 that need no model
```

### Evals

```bash
python evals/run_all.py            # writes evals/REPORT.md
python evals/run_all.py --no-cache # ignore cached responses
```

Exits non-zero if detection integrity drops below 1.000, so it can gate a pipeline. Cached model answers are keyed on the Ollama version, so the first run after Ollama updates itself regenerates everything: about 85 minutes for every eval set on this laptop. The quality numbers deliberately do **not** gate — a threshold on them would only invite tuning to the threshold.

The test suite hits a real Ollama server rather than a mock for the LLM tests, on purpose: the claim under test — that the sampler cannot emit a value outside our enum — is a claim about Ollama's behaviour. A mock would only test our belief about it.

### Test data

`evals/golden/build_synthetic_policy.py` generates a 39-clause IRDAI-style policy spanning all seven clause types, plus a ground-truth label file. The PDF and the labels come from the same source, so they cannot drift apart.

It also generates a **hostile variant**: the identical policy typeset with one font, no bold, and clause numbers run inline — the way many machine-generated policy PDFs actually arrive. The segmenter must extract the same 39 clauses from both. That fixture exists because the original test suite passed 14/14 while being circular: the thresholds had been tuned on the very document they were tested against.

No real insurer policy wordings are committed to this repository.

## Not advice

This tool produces an automated reading of a document. It is **not legal or financial advice**, it is not a substitute for reading your policy, and it may be wrong. Always confirm anything that matters with your insurer in writing.
